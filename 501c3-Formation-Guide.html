Organization Basics

Enter your organization's name, address, and key details

✓
▼

Here's what each field means and how to fill it in:

Organization Name * Your full legal name. Most states require "Inc.", "Incorporated", "Corporation", or "Corp." at the end. Check your state's rules.
DBA / Trade Name Optional "Doing Business As" name — the name you use publicly if different from your legal name. Most small nonprofits leave this blank.
Nonprofit Purpose * Choose the type of nonprofit: Public Benefit (most common — serving the community), Mutual Benefit (serving members), or Religious. Most nonprofits choose "Public Benefit."
Specific Purpose Clause * A clear 1–3 sentence description of what your organization does. This exact language appears in your Articles and IRS application. Be specific — not just "charitable purposes."
Principal Address * Your organization's main mailing address. Can be a home address for new nonprofits. Enter street, city, state, and ZIP separately.
Fiscal Year End * When your "tax year" ends. December 31 is the default and most common choice. Your annual 990 return is due 4.5 months after this date.
Registered Agent * A person or company that receives legal notices for your org. Must have a physical address in your state. Often a board member for small nonprofits.
Expected Annual Revenue * Choose Under $50K if you expect to be small. This determines which IRS form you file: 1023-EZ ($275, simpler) or full 1023 ($600, more detailed).
Public Charity Type * Most new nonprofits choose 509(a)(1) — a "publicly supported" charity that gets donations from the public. Leave at default unless you're sure you need something different.
Phone & Website Optional contact information for your organization. Can be added later once you have them.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I need an account? Is it really free? ▼

Yes, completely free — no credit card, no trial period, no hidden costs. The account requirement serves two purposes: it lets your progress be saved and restored reliably across sessions, and it allows Build Your Club to notify you about updates, new resources, and guidance relevant to your nonprofit formation journey. Creating an account takes under 60 seconds (name, email, password). You can sign out at any time using the Sign Out button in the top-right corner of the app.

Is my information safe? Who can see what I enter? ▼

Your formation data (organization name, board member names, addresses, purpose clause, etc.) is stored only in your own browser's local storage — it does not leave your device or get uploaded to any server. Only your account registration details (name, email, organization, phone) are visible to Build Your Club administrators. Your information is never sold or shared with third parties. You agreed to this when you checked the consent box at registration, as clearly stated in the registration form.

I forgot my password. How do I reset it? ▼

Contact Build Your Club at skycopatc@yahoo.com and request a password reset. Include the email address you used to register. An administrator will reset your access. Because this is a free tool with local-browser data storage, we recommend noting your password in a secure place (such as a password manager) when you create your account.

Do I need to hire a lawyer to form a nonprofit? ▼

Not necessarily. Many small nonprofits are formed without legal help using tools like this app. A lawyer is most useful if your situation is complex — for example, if you're converting an existing business, have international activities, expect significant assets, or have a complicated governance structure. For a straightforward community nonprofit, this app plus careful reading of IRS and state instructions is usually sufficient. If you're unsure, a one-hour consultation with a nonprofit attorney (typical cost: $150–$300) can give you peace of mind without breaking the bank.

Can I pay myself (the founder) a salary? ▼

Yes — nonprofit founders can be paid employees. However, there are strict rules. Your compensation must be "reasonable" (meaning comparable to what similar organizations pay for similar work). The board — not you — must set and approve your salary, and the decision should be documented in board minutes. You cannot sit on the board and vote on your own compensation. Excessive compensation can trigger IRS audits and jeopardize your 501(c)(3) status. Most small nonprofits in their first 1–2 years pay their founders little or nothing until they have stable funding.

How long does IRS approval take? ▼

Form 1023-EZ: Typically 2–4 weeks. This is the simplified form available to smaller organizations (projected gross receipts under $50,000/year, assets under $250,000).

Form 1023 (full): Typically 3–6 months, sometimes longer during busy periods. Incomplete applications can cause significant delays. Good news: once approved, your tax-exempt status is retroactive to your incorporation date — donations made during the waiting period are still deductible for your donors.

Can I run the nonprofit from my home address? ▼

Legally, yes — but with caution. Your address becomes part of the public record when you file Articles of Incorporation. Anyone can look it up. Many founders use a P.O. box, a UPS Store mailbox, or a registered agent address as the organization's public address. A registered agent service typically costs $50–$150/year and keeps your home address private. If you use your home as the registered agent address, be aware it will appear on state corporate records permanently.

What's the difference between a public charity and a private foundation? ▼

Both are 501(c)(3) organizations, but they're treated differently by the IRS. A public charity is supported by a broad base of donors, grants, and/or fees — most community nonprofits qualify. A private foundation is typically funded by one person, family, or corporation and makes grants to other organizations. Public charity status is generally better: higher deduction limits for donors, less IRS scrutiny, more grant eligibility. This app is designed for public charities. If your organization will be primarily funded by a single donor or family, consult an attorney about private foundation rules.

Do I need to file anything with the IRS every year? ▼

Yes. All 501(c)(3) organizations must file an annual information return with the IRS, even if they had no income. Which form depends on your size:

  • Form 990-N (e-Postcard): Gross receipts normally ≤ $50,000. Takes 5 minutes online. Free.
  • Form 990-EZ: Gross receipts $50,001–$200,000 and assets < $500,000.
  • Form 990 (full): Gross receipts ≥ $200,000 or assets ≥ $500,000.

Missing 3 consecutive filings causes automatic revocation of your 501(c)(3) status — a serious problem requiring IRS reinstatement. The app's Key Dates & Reminders section will help you track this deadline.

Can our nonprofit collect donations before IRS approval? ▼

Yes — you can accept donations immediately after incorporating. You just need to be transparent with donors that your 501(c)(3) application is pending. Once approved, your status is retroactive to your incorporation date, meaning donations made during the waiting period are fully deductible for your donors. Keep detailed records of all donations received before approval. Do not promise a tax deduction until the IRS confirmation is in hand — though you can truthfully say "our application is pending and approval is retroactive."

⚠️ Top 5 Mistakes to Avoid

1
A Vague or Wrong Purpose Clause
The IRS carefully reviews your purpose clause. Phrases like "to help the community" or "to do good works" are rejected. You need language that mirrors Section 501(c)(3): "organized exclusively for charitable, educational, religious, or scientific purposes." Also avoid listing purposes that could benefit private individuals — the IRS looks for exclusively public benefit. Use the purpose clause field in Step 2 of the app and keep it specific: what you do, for whom, and how it serves the public.
2
Not Enough Directors (or All From One Family)
Most states require a minimum of 3 directors — some require more (check Step 3 in the app for your state's requirement). Beyond the legal minimum, the IRS scrutinizes boards where a majority of directors are related by blood, marriage, or employment. A board dominated by family members raises "insider control" concerns that can delay or deny your application. Aim for independent, unrelated directors who genuinely represent your community.
3
Skipping the Registered Agent
Every state requires a registered agent — a person or company in your state who can receive legal documents on behalf of the organization during business hours. This is not optional. If you skip it, your Articles will be rejected. If your registered agent becomes unreachable (you moved, changed jobs, etc.) and the state can't serve legal papers, your corporate status can be administratively dissolved. Update your registered agent whenever it changes — it's a simple state filing, usually under $25.
4
Mixing Personal and Organizational Finances
One of the fastest ways to lose your nonprofit status — or expose yourself to personal liability — is to mix personal and organizational funds. Open a dedicated bank account in the organization's name as soon as you have your EIN. Never run organizational income or expenses through a personal account, pay personal expenses from the nonprofit account, or accept donations into a personal account. The IRS calls this "private benefit" and "inurement" — both are grounds for revocation.
5
Incorporating in the Wrong State
Many first-time founders hear that Delaware is "great for businesses" and choose to incorporate there. For a small community nonprofit, this is usually a mistake. If you operate in Minnesota but incorporate in Delaware, you'll typically need to register as a foreign nonprofit in Minnesota anyway — paying fees in both states. Unless you have a specific reason (multi-state operations, specific legal protections you've researched), incorporate in your home state. That's where you operate, where your members are, and where it's simplest to maintain compliance.
